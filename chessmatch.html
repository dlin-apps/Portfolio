CHESSMATCH

Learning chess by playing something you already know.

ChessMatch is a match-3 puzzle game that teaches you the chess pieces, and more importantly how each one moves, without ever feeling like a lesson. It borrows the familiarity of match-3 as an on-ramp to a game a lot of beginners find intimidating.

Why

The On-Ramp Problem

I love chess, and I kept running into the same thing: people are curious about it but bounce off the on-ramp. The first wall for a beginner isn't strategy; it's simply remembering the six pieces and how each one moves. Traditional teaching front-loads that as memorization before you're allowed to have any fun. I wanted the opposite: let the fun come first, and let the learning happen as a side effect of play.

The Bet

Make the Pieces the Mechanic

Match-3 is one of the most familiar mechanics in mobile gaming; almost everyone has played something like it. That familiarity is the asset. So instead of bolting a tutorial onto the front, I made the pieces and their movement the core mechanic itself.

How It Teaches

The Power-Up Is the Lesson

Matching gems in particular shapes spawns a chess piece on the board. When you trigger that piece, it clears cells in the exact pattern of how that piece moves in real chess. A player isn't memorizing a diagram; they're using a rook to win and watching it sweep a line, over and over, until "rook = straight lines" is simply intuition. The power-up and the lesson are the same object.

The teaching mechanic in one view: each piece clears the board exactly how it moves. Rooks sweep lines, bishops the diagonals, knights the L-jump, and so on.

On a live board, larger matches leave specials behind. Here a pawn, a rook and a bishop are sitting among the gems, each ready to clear in its own pattern.

The in-game instructions keep it to one honest rule: "Tap a piece and it moves the way that piece moves, and clears the same squares." The mechanic is the explanation.

Design Decisions

A Few Decisions Worth Calling Out

  • Chaining rewards recognition. A piece caught in another piece's blast triggers too, so clever placements set off cascades, nudging players to anticipate each piece's reach, which is exactly the mental model chess requires.
  • The goal is always legible. Objectives live in a clear display: a "Goal" label, progress shown as collected over target, and a check when complete, so a good move always has a visible payoff.
  • Difficulty as a teaching ramp. Early levels use fewer colors and more moves so pieces appear often and there's room to experiment; later levels tighten both.

Later levels add ice as an obstacle, and a one-line hint frames the strategy ("big matches are the way through") rather than dictating it.

Tuning the curve also surfaced two levels that were literally unwinnable: they asked for a gem color that couldn't spawn at that stage. A useful reminder that in a teaching game, "unfair" doesn't just frustrate; it breaks trust in the lesson.

Status

Where It Stands

A playable build with 20 hand-tuned levels, the full piece-spawning and chain-activation system, objectives, and a win/lose loop.

What's next: a light onboarding that names each piece the first time it appears; an economy sink for the coins you earn; and the real test, learning transfer. Do players who've internalized the clear patterns actually recognize the moves at a real chessboard? That's the metric that decides whether the core bet worked, and it's what I'd want to playtest next.

Reflection

What I Took From It

The part I'm proudest of is the decision to make the mechanic be the curriculum rather than wrap a curriculum in a mechanic. It's a small idea with a nice property: the thing that makes the game fun (powerful, satisfying clears) is the same thing that teaches. The honest open question isn't "is it fun," it's "does the knowledge transfer off the screen."
