The Challenge

Designing for Workflows, Not Products

Our users don't think in products; they think of Axon as a whole. A single incident can start in 911 Dispatch, continue with body, car, and drone cameras livestreaming and coordinating on the scene, and end in Records and Evidence Management, in the car or at the station, where officers summarize what happened and prepare for court.

One of the biggest contributions my team and I made was designing for those end-to-end workflows instead of for individual products. We mapped them across products and contexts, including where the major pain points were.

Axon Records

Axon Records was a new, complex product line we built from scratch: the public safety equivalent of health records in medicine. It had to handle local standards for coding and capturing incidents; federal, state, and local databases of people, vehicles, and locations; and workflows that run from Patrol Officers and Records Clerks to Law Enforcement Leadership, Investigators, Prosecutors and Defenders in court, and the public.

911 Dispatch, from the Ground Up

We also built a 911 dispatch product line from the ground up: all the software used when a 911 call comes in, to gather information, decide which resources to send, and coordinate everyone heading to and on the scene, including real-time audio and video. It is a very high-stress situation, so every application and workflow has to be easy and intuitive. A hard-to-use or inconsistent experience can quite literally mean people die.

The Axon Design System

I built the first design system at Axon and created a Design Operations team to expand and evolve it over time. The Axon Design System governed interaction, visual, and workflow design and best practices across all Axon products, on mobile, in-car/tablet, and desktop, in both light and dark themes to suit the context.

Designers used it in Figma as a library with version control and usage metrics across 40+ Product Designers; engineers used a component library built and evolved by a central Engineering team. We set adoption goals for every product line, reviewed them monthly, and reached around 80% adoption across product lines.

Building the Org

From a Handful of Designers to 60+

Behind the work was the organization. Our UX mission had three focus areas: a strategic one, building great products and a connected ecosystem; a tactical one, maturing the UX disciplines; and an operational one, building the best team and culture. We set yearly and quarterly goals for each.

Org Design

Over five years I grew the UX org from a few Product Designers to 60+ people, and grew people from mid and senior levels into Director roles. Each product line got a full UX team with a UX leader and a level of management under them. I introduced UX Research to the company, first as embedded researchers organized horizontally and later under the UX Directors of each product line to align on a common mission and goals. I built a central Technical Writing and User Assistance org and grew a leader for that discipline, and a Design System and Operations team that designed and built everything common across products and UX teams. Org design is one of my personal passions, especially matrix structures that balance deep vertical execution with horizontal discipline building.

Career Ladders and Hiring

I established and evolved career ladders for Product Design, UX Research, and Technical Writing, so everyone had clear career goals and knew how to work toward them. We were the first team at the company to complete and use them. I also set up our hiring approach, process, and content, including how other disciplines took part in hiring UX team members, to keep a high bar. I hired 60+ people onto the UX team directly and was on the hiring team for 150+ more in disciplines like Product Management and Engineering.

Mechanisms for Quality

With a large UX team across many product lines, and user workflows that span Axon and third-party products, the experience had to be high quality within each product and across them. We developed 3 mechanisms and 9 types of reviews covering everything from the strategic platform vision down to the smallest details, including how to collaborate with every discipline and involve key stakeholders up to the C-level executive team. We kept evolving them as the company and portfolio changed, and kept them flexible so they never slowed us down or started to feel like the work itself instead of a way to keep our eye on users.
